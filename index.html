Don’t take the bait: phishing awareness

Phishing is when an attacker pretends to be someone you trust to steal your passwords, money, or access. This short module shows you how to spot it, with an example to inspect and a quiz at the end.

What phishing looks like

Email phishing
Mass messages pretending to be a bank, delivery service, or software company.
Spear phishing
Targeted at one person, using real details about their job or contacts.
Smishing and vishing
The same trick by text message (SMS) or phone call.
Fake websites
Copies of real login pages that send your password to the attacker.

How attackers manipulate you

This is social engineering: attacking the person, not the computer. Most scams pull one of these levers:

Urgency
“Act in 24 hours.” Pressure stops you thinking.
Fear
“Your account was hacked.” Panic makes you click.
Authority
A message that looks like it’s from your boss, IT, or the government.
Reward
“You won a prize.” Greed or curiosity lowers your guard.

Spot a fake website

Read the web address from right to left, up to the first single slash. The real domain is the part just before it.

northbank.com/loginReal: the domain is northbank.com
northbank.com.secure-login.netFake: the real domain is secure-login.net
northbnk.comFake: a letter is missing
northbank-support.xyzFake: extra words and an odd ending

A padlock only means the connection is encrypted. Scam sites can have one too.

Real cases

Fake invoices, 2013 to 2015
A scammer posed as a hardware supplier and sent invoices that two large tech companies paid, losing over $100 million in total. Nobody checked the payment details by another channel.
Twitter, 2020
Attackers phoned employees pretending to be IT, got them to enter credentials on a fake login page, and took over high-profile accounts. The weak point was people, not software.

How to protect yourself

  • Pause. Urgent messages deserve more suspicion, not less.
  • Check the sender’s real address, not just the display name.
  • Preview links before clicking (hover on a computer, long-press on a phone).
  • Go to the site yourself by typing the address or using the official app.
  • Never share passwords or one-time codes with anyone who contacts you.
  • Turn on multi-factor authentication and use a password manager.
  • Don’t open unexpected attachments.
  • Report suspicious messages to your IT or security team.

If you already clicked

Don’t panic, act quickly: change the password on the real site, turn on multi-factor authentication, tell your IT or security team, and watch your accounts for unusual activity. If you entered the same password anywhere else, change it there too.
